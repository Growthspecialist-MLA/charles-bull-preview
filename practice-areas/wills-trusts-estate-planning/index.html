Wills & Trusts · Welsh, Louisiana

Wills, Trusts & Estate Planning Attorney in Welsh, Louisiana

Everyone who owns a home, a bank account or a vehicle has an estate. A plan decides who receives it and who can help you if you can't manage on your own.

Estate planning is how you protect the people you love. A solid plan says who receives your property, who handles your affairs if you become ill, and what medical care you want. Without one, Louisiana law and the courts make those decisions for you.

With more than 45 years of practice in Louisiana, Charles Bull prepares wills, trusts, powers of attorney and healthcare directives for families across Southwest Louisiana. He listens first, then builds the plan around what matters most to you.

Wills

A will states how you want your property distributed after your death and who should handle your estate. In Louisiana, a will is called a testament. The most common kind, a notarial testament, must be in writing, dated, and signed by you, two witnesses and a notary. (La. C.C. art. 1576, opens in a new tab) A will that misses a requirement can fail entirely, and then your wishes are not followed. Louisiana simplified these rules in August 2025, but careful drafting still matters: a properly completed will can be proved in court without calling the witnesses.

Louisiana is different

Louisiana inheritance law comes from the civil law tradition. Terms like testament, legatee and usufruct, and rules about community property and forced heirship, have no exact match in other states. Charles drafts every document for Louisiana law.

Forced heirship

Louisiana reserves part of a parent's estate for certain children, called forced heirs. A forced heir is a child who is 23 or younger when the parent dies, or a child of any age who permanently cannot care for themselves because of a mental or physical condition. One forced heir is entitled to one-fourth of the estate; two or more share one-half. (La. C.C. art. 1493, opens in a new tab) A will that ignores forced heirship can be challenged.

Trusts

A trust is set up during your lifetime under the Louisiana Trust Code. You transfer property to a trustee, who manages it under the instructions you write, and at your death the trustee distributes it to your beneficiaries. A living trust must be created by authentic act, or by a signed document executed before two witnesses and acknowledged. (La. R.S. 9:1752, opens in a new tab)

  • Trust property can pass to beneficiaries without going through a succession.
  • You can name who manages your property if you become unable to.
  • You can set conditions on when and how beneficiaries receive property.

Not every family needs a trust, and not all trusts work the same way. Charles explains which tools fit your situation and why.

Planning for incapacity

A complete plan covers more than what happens at death. A durable power of attorney lets someone you trust handle your finances, and a healthcare power of attorney and living will guide your medical care if you cannot speak for yourself. If someone in your family has received a dementia diagnosis, see Alzheimer's and dementia planning.

What a plan does for your family

  • Your property goes to the people you choose.
  • Someone you trust can act for you if you become ill.
  • Your family avoids guesswork and conflict while they are grieving.
  • Settling your estate can take less time and cost less.

Why does Louisiana estate planning work differently?

Louisiana's inheritance law comes from the civil law tradition, not the common law used in the other 49 states. The terms, the signing formalities and some of the rules about who must inherit are different, so documents drafted for another state may not work as intended here.

What happens if I die without a will in Louisiana?

Louisiana law decides who inherits, not a judge. Children inherit in equal shares. A surviving spouse generally keeps the use of the deceased spouse's half of the community property until death or remarriage, and receives it outright if there are no children. That may not match what you would have chosen, and your family will usually still need a succession.

Do I need a trust, or is a will enough?

It depends on your property, your family and your goals. A will takes effect at death and passes through a succession. A trust is set up during your life and can hold property for your beneficiaries. Charles explains the trade-offs for your situation.

What else belongs in an estate plan besides a will?

Most plans also include a durable power of attorney for finances, a healthcare power of attorney and a living will. These cover the years before death, when you may need someone to act for you.
